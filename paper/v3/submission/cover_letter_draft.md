We submit the research paper "Do knowledge-tracing simulators reward learning? A negative-control audit of reinforcement-learning exercise recommendation for programming" for consideration in *Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence*.

Adaptive exercise recommenders increasingly combine knowledge-tracing (KT) models with reinforcement learning (RL), and because studies with learners are costly, the resulting policies are often trained and evaluated inside the fitted KT model. Using real logs of a C-programming online judge and protocols locked before the evaluation population was loaded, we show that the gains such evaluations report can be large for policies that teach nothing: in a negative-control world without any learning, the DKT tracker still reported substantial post-test gains, and across all evaluated policies in-simulator gains were almost perfectly rank-correlated with these phantom gains. We prove that the expected gain inside a KT simulator equals the model's accumulated self-predicted change (drift), and we propose inexpensive checks—negative-control worlds, drift reporting and independently fitted worlds—that any simulator-based evaluation of an adaptive recommender can adopt.

The paper fits the journal's scope on personalised and intelligent educational systems and on applying AI models to educational data. All policy outcomes are simulated and clearly reported as such; we make no claim about learning gains of real students.
